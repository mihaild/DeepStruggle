P44 diagnostics · 13–14 Nov 2026 · step 1 control (E8.1-A8-R119-S45) and J1 (E8-A8-R102-S44)

Why self-play drops good strategies

Both runs keep improving, yet they abandon setups a strong human would keep (West Germany for the US, Yugoslavia or Austria for the USSR) and do not come back. The diagnostics point to four causes, one of them new: the critic has learned to rate play unlike its own as losing, by 3–13 win-rate points, even when the unfamiliar play comes from a stronger network.

Summary

  1. The drift is real and long. J1's US left West Germany for 7,200M steps; the control's USSR held Yugoslavia/Austria only 15% of its run, J1's 45%. Switches happen within 40–80M.
  2. It is drift, not cycling. Round robins of both runs are fully transitive: every checkpoint beats every earlier one.
  3. For the network's own play the abandoned setups are worth the same (within about 1 point). It never learned the multi-turn plans that make them valuable, so self-play has no reason to choose them.
  4. The critic penalises unfamiliar play. Single in-game deviations are judged correctly, but when a whole turn is played in another style, the critic rates that side 3–13 points worse than the playouts show, even for stronger exploiters. The source is the opponent pool: the side that plays unlike the current self is usually an older snapshot, and it loses 62% of the time. A branch whose value trains on self-play games only lost the error within 390M, and within 770M its USSR was placing in Austria in every deal (the human Pol 4 Aus 1 EG 1 in 17%), at no cost in strength.
  5. The training signal for early decisions is almost all critic. The game result reaches a setup decision with weight ~0.2%; the rest is the critic's bootstrap. So the critic's familiarity bias decides early choices, and it locks in whatever is played.

Drift, not cycling

Round robins of 400M SWAs every 800M (control 4,000–10,400M, J1 6,000–13,600M, 1,000 deals a side): 0 intransitive triples of 84 and 120; no older checkpoint beats a newer one; the newer side's score grows with the gap, from +1 point at 800M to +10–12 at 6,400M. Strength rises slowly and steadily while strategies wander along directions that do not change the result against the current self.

How a setup gets dropped

That is a two-state system with no preference between the states, and noise 3–7 times larger than any steady term carries it across. The advantages behind those policy-gradient terms come from the critic, which is where the next section starts.

The critic rates unfamiliar play as losing

Single decisions: correct

At 2,000 in-game positions and 4,000 turn-1 AR1–AR2 decisions, the critic's value after the policy's preferred move and after a less likely legal move matches 32 playouts per move: habit gap +0.05 ± 0.2 win-rate points, in every slice (side, action round, decision type, rarity).

Setup placements: wrong, and in favour of what is played

  • Control: an extra Poland point undervalued by 4–7 points per placement, at 7,600M and 8,000M; whole forced setups rated 2–3 points in favour of whichever setup the policy currently plays, flipping when it switches.
  • J1: the habitual placement favoured by 0.7–1.3 points on average (3.5–6.5 SE). Yugoslavia/Austria is ~1 point better than its habitual Hungary point in play; the critic rates it 2.5 points worse. After the switch it credits Canada over West Germany/Italy/Iran by 2 points that the playouts do not show.

A whole turn played in another style: wrong by 3–13 points

Turn 1 (setup included) is played by another network for one side, then the late network plays both sides to the end, 800 positions × 24 playouts per row. The error grows with how different the style is, not with how weak it is: the exploiters make their side better and are still marked down 4 points. Per-position error beyond playout noise is 6–10 points even on familiar positions, and calibration slopes stay near 1, so this is an offset by style, not a scale problem.

Where it comes from (confirmed by the R124 branch)

30% of training games are against older pool snapshots, which lose 55–73% of their games to the learner (62% on average). The value loss covers every row, including the pool opponent's own decisions, and the critic has no input saying who is playing. The best predictor it can learn is "the side that plays unlike the current self is a pool member and will lose". That is right on the training mixture and wrong whenever the policy asks "what if I play this differently". An old setup is exactly such a signature, which explains why returning to one looks losing and why the critic's setup preference flips together with the policy.

A second, pool-free source was possible (at temperature 1, a side that drew rare moves really did play worse). The diagnostic branch rules it out as the main one: training the value on self-play games only removed the style error within 390M (plan step 1).

Deep reasons

1. The critic reads opponent identity from style

With a hidden opponent type, a critic without the type learns the mixture value, so style is evidence of the weak type. Off-Belief Learning (Hu et al., 2021) and ReBeL (Brown et al., 2020) describe values that read meaning into who took past actions; KataGo's 2020 run hit the same problem with unequal playouts and fixed it by telling the network which side was stronger. Nothing found documents it in pool self-play; the handover numbers above are that posterior, measured.

2. Strategic indifference: self-play only improves along the transitive axis

Balduzzi et al. (2019) and Czarnecki et al. (2020): directions that do not change the result against the current self are not learned, they drift. The network never acquired the plans that make West Germany or the Italy route pay, so it cannot see their value; each setup is judged by a policy tuned to the other.

3. On-policy gradients commit, and dropped options return slowly

Chung et al. (2021) and Mei et al. (2021): sampled actions with positive advantage get reinforced, and normalised policy gradients can lock onto a suboptimal option. NeuRD (Hennes et al., 2019): the softmax gradient scales with π(a), so an option at 1% barely moves. Per-step entropy and the zero-avoiding term add decision noise, not strategic exploration (Osband et al., 2016).

4. Early decisions learn almost only from the critic

With λ = 0.98 over ~300 decisions, the game result reaches a setup's target with weight ~0.2% (Schulman et al., 2016: λ < 1 is biased exactly when the critic is). Learned critics fit poorly where the policy rarely goes (Ilyas et al., 2020), and with a shared trunk policy and value interfere (Cobbe et al., 2021, PPG). So reason 1's bias becomes the setup's training signal.

5. The KL term is a trust region, not R-NaD

R-NaD and NashPG converge only because each reference is held until the regularised game is solved (Perolat et al., 2021; DeepNash η 0.2 with references held 10–100k steps; NashPG paper 10,000 updates per reference). Ours refreshes πref every 200k environment steps (~50 updates) at η 0.1, so nothing anchors indifferent directions and the last iterate wanders (Mertikopoulos et al., 2018). With the round robins transitive, this is a contributor, not the main cause.

2. Give the critic the opponent type needs owner: architecture

R124 itself is a working interim: same architecture, one flag, no strength cost so far. Its known flaw is that pool games' advantages are computed by a critic that never trained on them, which biases those games' policy gradient; the group heads remove that too. Choosing between continuing R124 as an arm and building the heads is the owner's call.

One value head per opponent group (self-play, own pool, league), or a critic-only input with the same information. Every row of a game uses that game's head (the zero-sum GAE bootstraps through the opponent's rows), and the self-play head is trained on self-play rows. This is the supported fix (AlphaStar's privileged value, asymmetric critics; Baisero & Amato 2022 show a critic that also sees what the agent sees stays unbiased). Not an observation change: the policy reads nothing new. Success: handover error and setup habit bias at the familiar level (under 1 point), and the "held setup reinforced" term in the gradient split shrinks.

3. Do it before P45's league sequencing

A seat-true league adds opponents whose style marks them too; without group heads the critic would fold their win rates into the same shortcut, whichever way they win. The league is still the only mechanism here that makes a weak strategy cost something (AlphaStar's league ablation: +280 Elo over mains alone), so it follows, it is not dropped.

4. Show the critic other game families

Exploring starts as part of the environment: in a share of games, both sides' setups (and possibly turn 1) are drawn from a menu of the run's own earlier lines, human setups and one-point perturbations, with those decisions kept out of the policy loss and the value trained on the games (Go-Exploit, Trudeau & Bowling 2023; KataGo's branched openings). This is critic data, not a teacher: it does not say which setup is right. Measured by the same probes; the question is whether the policy then mixes among equal setups instead of locking.

5. Only if drift persists after 2–4

  • A faithful R-NaD schedule in one arm: η 0.2, πref held ~40M steps.
  • A Monte Carlo value target at setup states (λ = 1 there), which removes the 0.2% chain; the opponent heads may make it unnecessary.
  • As a tie-breaker, not a cure: a small KL to the human setup distribution on setup rows (DiL-πKL, Bakhtin et al. 2022). If setups really are within a point, it costs nothing in self-play.

Not recommended: more per-step entropy, λ 0.99 globally (collapsed on E4), PSRO/NeuPL at one GPU (Rudolph et al., 2025: no gain over tuned PPO).
